01 — The idea

Two antennas, two paths, one difference.

A passive radar node has two ears. One points at a distant broadcast tower and listens to its signal head-on. Call that the reference. The other faces the open sky and catches the very same signal a beat later, after it has glanced off a passing aircraft. Call that the echo. The echo always takes the long way round, and that extra stretch of travel is the aircraft's bistatic range.

Grab the aircraft and move it around the sky. Watch the two paths stretch, and see how much farther the echo has to travel.

Range is a time difference

The node never measures distance directly. It measures time: how many millionths of a second later the echo lands, compared with the signal that came straight from the tower. Radio travels at the speed of light, so that sliver of delay converts cleanly into distance. It's the whole echo path, minus the straight shot from tower to node.

Why "bistatic"?

The transmitter and the receiver sit in different spots (bi-static), where an ordinary radar packs both into one dish. RETINA owns neither: it quietly borrows a tower that's already out there broadcasting.

02 — Speed for free

A moving echo changes pitch.

You already know this one. A siren climbs in pitch as the ambulance races toward you, then drops the instant it passes. Motion crams the waves together up front and spreads them out behind. Radio does exactly the same thing: a moving reflector nudges the echo's frequency up or down, and the node reads that nudge in hertz. It's a free speedometer for anything in the sky.

Set a speed and send the aircraft past. Coming toward the node reads positive, heading away reads negative, and right at the closest point it passes through zero. Faster planes swing the shift wider.

A few hundred hertz

The shift is minuscule next to the carrier: a couple hundred hertz riding on top of, say, a 200 MHz FM signal. That's about one part in a million. Vanishingly small, and still more than enough to clock a plane's speed.

03 — The core plot

Every detection is a single dot.

Now lay the two clues side by side. Distance comes from the delay; speed comes from the Doppler shift. Plot one against the other and you have the single most useful picture in passive radar: the delay-Doppler plot. Left to right is Doppler (how fast the gap is closing); bottom to top is range (how far off). A whole aircraft, in one frozen instant, lands as one dot.

There are two things to play with here. Drag the plane to move it through the sky. Drag the small round handle to swing its heading, the direction it's actually flying. The dot on the right is what the node sees.

One thing tends to surprise people. Aim the plane straight along the line of sight and the Doppler leaps to the edge of the plot. Turn it broadside, flying across, and the shift almost disappears, at the very same speed. The plot only cares how fast the distance is changing, not how fast the plane is really moving.

04 — Ten seconds of sky

Hold the dots, and a flight path appears.

One dot is a single heartbeat. Keep a rolling trail of them, a view the node calls a max hold, and the aircraft signs its name. Up top you see the plane physically fly past; below, in perfect step, is the track it leaves on the plot. It sweeps in from the top as the plane approaches, dives to its closest point right as the Doppler crosses 0 Hz, then peels away again, painted in the same colors the live map uses for real echoes.

The closest point of approach

It's the horn-past-the-window moment. The pitch flips from high to low exactly as the car draws level, and the track flips from its approaching half to its receding half at the closest point. Nudge the speed and the miss distance and watch the whole signature change shape.

05 — What one node can't tell you

One range, a whole curve of maybes.

Range tells you how far. It says nothing about which way. Measure 15 km and the aircraft could be anywhere along a curve of equal-distance points wrapped around you. That curve isn't a circle, though. Because the tower and the node sit apart, every point with the same echo-time traces an ellipse, with the tower and your node sitting at its two focal points.

A single ear can't choose between the points on that ellipse. A directional antenna helps: it only listens to a wedge of sky, shrinking the answer to the slice of ellipse inside the beam. Steer the beam onto the plane and it lights up.

Steer the beam

What the node measures is bistatic range: the full trip, the blue leg plus the amber leg, minus the green baseline. Hold that total fixed and the aircraft could sit anywhere on the grey ellipse. Drag the plane anywhere and the ellipse redraws through it, tracing every other point the node can't tell it apart from. That's the ambiguity: one number, a whole curve of maybes. A directional antenna narrows it to a wedge of sky with a limited reach. Drag the beam's aim and width knobs over the plane until it glows.

Why an ellipse?

Add up the echo's two legs, tower to plane (blue) plus plane to node (amber), and hold that total fixed. The set of points that satisfy it is the very definition of an ellipse, with the tower and node as its two foci. A lone dish radar draws circles; a borrowed-transmitter radar draws ellipses.

06 — Pinning it down

Three ways to break the ambiguity.

One node and one tower still leave you with that ellipse of doubt. RETINA shrinks it to a single point three ways: by matching against aircraft that announce themselves, by teaming up two nodes, and soon by listening to several towers at once.

Matches an ADS-B aircraft Unexplained detection

Subtract what you already know

Most aircraft are chatty. Their transponders announce exactly where they are, on a public feed called ADS-B. RETINA lays that honest list of planes over its own detections. Every echo that matches a known aircraft is confirmed and set aside. Whatever is left has some explaining to do.

Six detections, five of them matched to transponder-equipped aircraft.

Not everything squawks

ADS-B isn't the whole story. Plenty of military and older aircraft stay silent. An echo with no ADS-B twin isn't proof of anything strange, but it's the exact thing a passive radar exists to catch: whatever chooses not to announce itself.

Drag the aircraft and watch both nodes' range rings

Two rings, two crossings

Bring in a second node, on your roof or a neighbor's place a mile down the road. Now the plane has to sit on both nodes' range rings at once, and two rings only cross in a couple of spots. One crossing is the real aircraft. The other is a mirror-image ghost.

That last coin-flip gets settled with a scrap more information: which way the antennas point, or a reading from a third node. What's left is a single dot on the map. That's how a scattering of ears turns "how far" into "exactly where".

One node, many towers

The next trick drops the second node entirely. A single surveillance antenna can hear the echoes of several towers at the same time. Each tower draws its own ellipse of maybes, and where all of them overlap, there's the plane.

The bigger picture

Every node adds an ear to the network.

Alone, a single node reads distance and speed from radio it never paid for. Scatter dozens across a region and the guesswork collapses into clean tracks: a living, open map of the sky, stitched together from signals that were already crowding the air. Nothing transmitted, nothing licensed, nothing taken from the spectrum.
