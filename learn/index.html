01 — The idea

Two antennas, two paths, one difference.

A passive radar node receives two signals. The reference antenna points at a distant broadcast tower and listens to its signal head-on: that's the reference signal. The other antenna faces the open sky and catches the very same signal a beat later, after it has glanced off a passing aircraft — the surveillance antenna is used to pick up this echo, and what it delivers is the surveillance signal. The echo always takes the long way round, and that extra stretch of travel is the aircraft's bistatic range.

Grab the aircraft and move it around the sky. Watch the two paths stretch, and see how much farther the echo has to travel.

Range is a time difference

The node never measures distance directly. It measures time: how many millionths of a second later the echo lands, compared with the signal that came straight from the tower. Radio travels at the speed of light, so that sliver of delay converts cleanly into distance. It's the whole echo path, minus the straight shot from tower to node.

Why "bistatic"?

The transmitter and the receiver sit in different spots (bi-static), where an ordinary radar packs both into one dish. You own the receiver — the node, its reference antenna and its surveillance antenna are all yours. What you don't own is the transmitter: RETINA quietly borrows a tower that's already out there broadcasting.

02 — Speed for free

A moving echo changes pitch.

You already know this one. A siren climbs in pitch as the ambulance races toward you, then drops the instant it passes. Motion crams the waves together up front and spreads them out behind. Radio does exactly the same thing: a moving reflector nudges the echo's frequency up or down, and the node reads that nudge in hertz. It's a free speedometer for anything in the sky.

Set a speed and send the aircraft past. Coming toward the node reads positive, heading away reads negative, and right at the closest point it passes through zero. Faster planes swing the shift wider.

The node isn't listening to the whole sky, though. The surveillance antenna only sees a wedge of it, drawn here as the pale blue cone. Watch the aircraft enter and leave that cone: outside it there is no echo to shift, no tone, no reading. The plane is still up there. The node simply can't hear it.

A few hundred hertz

The shift is minuscule next to the carrier: a couple hundred hertz riding on top of, say, a 200 MHz FM signal. That's about one part in a million. Vanishingly small, and still more than enough to clock a plane's speed.

The two traces at the bottom of the scene are what the node's two antennas hold up against each other. The reference signal, from the reference antenna, is the tower's own broadcast arriving straight and unchanged. The surveillance signal, from the surveillance antenna, is that same broadcast after it has bounced off something moving. Squeezed together, the echo is closing; stretched apart, it's opening away. The gap between the two numbers is the Doppler shift.

03 — The core plot

Every detection is a single dot.

Now lay the two clues side by side. Distance comes from the delay; speed comes from the Doppler shift. Plot one against the other and you have the single most useful picture in passive radar: the delay-Doppler plot. Left to right is range (how far off); bottom to top is Doppler (how fast the gap is closing). A whole aircraft, in one frozen instant, lands as one dot.

Both things in the scene move. Grab the aircraft itself — the ringed glyph — and drag it anywhere in the sky. Then grab the AIM ring on the end of the arrow to swing its heading, the direction it's actually flying. Move one, then the other, and watch the single dot below chase both.

One thing tends to surprise people. Aim the plane straight along a leg and the dot shoots to the top or bottom of the plot. Turn it broadside and it settles back onto the middle line, at the very same speed. The plot only cares how fast the total distance is changing, not how fast the plane is really moving.

Where 0 Hz actually is

Not perpendicular to the node — that's the answer for a normal radar, where the transmitter and receiver share one dish. Here the signal travels tower → plane → node, so the Doppler follows how fast both legs together are changing. It hits zero when the plane flies perpendicular to the bisector of the two legs, which is the same thing as flying along the equal-range ellipse. That's the cyan line in the scene: park the AIM knob on it and the readout reads 0.

04 — Ten seconds of sky

Hold the dots, and a flight path appears.

One dot is a single heartbeat. Keep a rolling trail of them, a view the node calls a max hold, and the aircraft signs its name. Up top you see the plane physically fly past; below, in perfect step, is the track it leaves on the plot. It sweeps in from far out as the plane approaches, swings down through 0 Hz at the closest point, then opens away again, painted in the same colors the live map uses for real echoes.

There's a catch the plot doesn't show you. A single range number isn't a place. It's a whole curve of maybes. Every point in the sky that sends its echo back after exactly the same delay lies on one ellipse, drawn with the tower and your node at its two focal points, and a lone antenna cannot tell any of those points apart. (A dish radar, transmitting and listening from the same spot, draws simple circles. Borrow someone else's transmitter and the circles stretch into ellipses.)

What narrows it down is the antenna itself. A directional antenna only listens to a wedge of sky, so the answer shrinks from a whole ellipse to the slice of it sitting inside the beam. Drag the AIM knob to point the wedge and set how far it reaches; drag WIDTH to open or close it. It starts 120° across. Now steer it off the flight path and watch the plot below: the track simply stops being drawn. No dots, no signature, no aircraft. As far as the node is concerned, nothing flew past at all.

The closest point of approach

It's the horn-past-the-window moment. The pitch flips from high to low exactly as the car draws level, and the track flips from its approaching half to its receding half at the closest point. Nudge the speed and the miss distance and watch the whole signature change shape.

The trade you're making

Pinch the surveillance beam narrow and every echo it does pick up is pinned to a small arc of sky, but the antenna is blind to everything either side of it. Open it wide and it covers far more sky, at the cost of knowing less about where any of it is. Real nodes live somewhere in the middle, and lean on the tricks in the next section to make up the difference.

05 — Pinning it down

One measurement, a whole arc of places.

Knowing roughly where the beam is looking is the easy half: the antenna's sensitivity falls away smoothly off boresight, so comparing how strong the same echo looks from moment to moment gives you a likely bearing with an error bar. The hard half is what happens inside the cone. Take the equal-range ellipse and keep only the part the beam can see, and you're left with an arc — a continuous curve across the cone, not a pair of alternatives. Every point on it is the same distance as far as the node is concerned, and for each one there's a heading that reproduces your measured Doppler exactly. Same range, same hertz, hundreds of different places.

Subtract what you already know

Most aircraft are chatty: their transponders announce exactly where they are, on a public feed called ADS-B. Laid over the map, most of what the node hears turns out to be traffic that was never in contention — crossed off because it sits outside the beam altogether. Of the candidates left on the arc, one has a transponder reporting from it. That's your detection, confirmed and named.

Every ADS-B report converts straight into the range and Doppler it would produce, so the whole feed gets drawn on the plot whether or not the node is hearing that aircraft. The hollow dashed markers are predictions with no echo behind them, because the beam isn't pointed at them. That's the normal working picture, and it makes the test simple: anything the node does hear that doesn't land on a predicted spot has some explaining to do.

Four of the six detections belong to aircraft outside the beam entirely.

Not everything squawks

Plenty of military and older aircraft stay silent. An echo with no ADS-B twin isn't proof of anything strange, but it's the exact thing a passive radar exists to catch: whatever chooses not to announce itself.

A second node cuts the arc

A second node, on your roof or a neighbour's place down the road, hears the same aircraft off the same tower and produces its own equal-range ellipse — drawn around its position, tilted differently. Your arc and node B's ellipse only agree where they cross. Node B has a surveillance antenna of its own too, shown in its own colour, swung onto the aircraft from a completely different angle.

One crossing, one aircraft.

On the plot, that one aircraft shows up as two dots. Node B sits somewhere else, so its path lengths and its closing rate are both different — same plane, different range, different hertz. Neither dot alone says where it is. The pair does.

When two curves cross twice

Loci this shape can meet in more than one place, and when they do you get a real aircraft and a mirror-image ghost that fits both measurements just as well. That coin-flip gets settled with a scrap more information: which way the antennas point, or a third node. Drag the aircraft around and you'll find positions where a ghost appears.

Two towers, one node

This one drops the second node entirely. A single surveillance antenna can hear the echoes of two towers at once, and the reason that works is that they broadcast on different frequencies: tune to 97.9 and you get one tower's echo, tune to 104.5 and you get the other's, out of the very same slice of recorded radio.

Two towers means two independent bistatic ranges, so a second ellipse — this one drawn around the second tower and your node. It cuts across your arc at a steep angle, and the crossing is the aircraft. One node, one surveillance antenna, a fix.

06 — Your turn

The Flight Path Doppler Simulator.

Everything so far has been on rails: a straight pass, a fixed speed, a signature that always comes out the same shape. This one is yours. Draw a flight path anywhere on the map — a straight run, a wide turn, a loop, an approach that hooks in and doubles back — then press play and watch the delay-Doppler plot below write out whatever that path sounds like to the node.

The beam is live here too. Steer it with the AIM and WIDTH knobs and the track breaks into fragments: the plot draws only the stretches your antenna could actually hear, exactly as a real node would record them. Try flying a long straight line through a narrow beam and see how little of the story survives.

Read your own track

A few things to try. Fly straight at the node and the track pins itself to the top of the plot and slides left as the range shrinks. Fly a circle around it and the range barely moves while the Doppler swings from one extreme to the other. Fly across the beam and you'll get a short, nearly flat smear near 0 Hz — fast aircraft, almost no Doppler, which is exactly the case that fools people.

The bigger picture

Every node adds an antenna to the network.

Alone, a single node reads distance and speed from radio it never paid for. Scatter dozens across a region and the guesswork collapses into clean tracks: a living, open map of the sky, stitched together from signals that were already crowding the air. Nothing transmitted, nothing licensed, nothing taken from the spectrum.
